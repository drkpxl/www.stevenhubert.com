CMH had been running on an aging Sitecore installation alongside the resort sites. It was the wrong tool for the company, and customers and CMH staff both felt it; loading the list of available heli-trips could take full minutes. The information architecture had been forced into a shape designed for a ski resort, and the click data showed customers struggling to find what they were looking for.

I led the discovery work with CMH and the agency team. We mapped flows for two very different audiences, first-time heli-ski guests and seasoned returning fliers, and rebuilt the content hierarchy to serve both without compromising either.

![CMH Home](/images/CMH/CMH-Home.png)
*Homepage of the new CMH site*

On the platform side I pushed for a [JAMstack](https://jamstack.org/) approach: [Contentful](https://www.contentful.com/) for the CMS, [Netlify](https://www.netlify.com/) for hosting and build, and [Jekyll](https://jekyllrb.com/) as the templating layer. The team had the technical depth to operate this stack, and the old monolith had taught us what we did not want to repeat.

![Booking Grid](/images/CMH/booking.png)
*The trip booking grid, the longest piece of design work in the project*

The grid had to feel familiar to returning customers while being far more usable than what they were used to. We also surfaced a hybrid weather feed at each lodge, mixing observed conditions from staff on the ground with automated station data, so guests got a clean read on what to expect before they booked.

The JAMstack choice let us do more on a smaller budget and ship a fast experience that finally matched what CMH is actually like to book.
